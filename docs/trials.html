FIND YOUR TRIAL

Find a clinical trial that is looking for volunteers

Clinical trials test new treatments, devices and rehabilitation programmes, and many of them are looking for patients and healthy volunteers. Search the official public registries of ongoing studies, then talk about what you find with your doctor.

Open your search in each registry (new tab). The same study can appear in more than one.

Where the studies are listed

ClinicalTrials.gov

The largest public registry, run by the US National Library of Medicine. It lists studies from many countries, not only the United States, including trials of medicines, devices, surgery and rehabilitation.

Study code looks like: NCT01234567

EU Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS)

The official European Union register, run by the European Medicines Agency. It covers clinical trials of medicines in the EU and EEA. Studies of devices, surgery or rehabilitation are usually not here.

Study code looks like: 2024-512345-12-00

ISRCTN registry

A primary registry recognised by the World Health Organization, based in the United Kingdom. It accepts studies of any type from any country.

Study code looks like: ISRCTN12345678

WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform

One search across many national registries at once, including registries in countries not covered above. Useful to double-check that you have not missed a study.

Before you contact a study

  1. Talk to your doctor first. Bring the study code: your doctor can tell you whether the study fits your situation and your current treatment.
  2. Read who can take part. Each study lists its eligibility criteria: who can join (inclusion) and who cannot (exclusion), such as age, diagnosis or other treatments.
  3. Check where and how often. Look at the study sites and how many visits are needed, and whether travel costs are covered.
  4. Ask the study team. What is being tested and compared? What would you have to do? What are the possible risks and benefits? What happens when the study ends?
  5. Take your time with the consent. Before joining you receive written information and sign an informed consent form. You can ask questions and talk with your family before you decide.
  6. You can always stop. Taking part is voluntary: you can withdraw at any time without giving a reason, and without losing your usual care.

Words you will meet

Recruiting
The study is looking for participants now.
Not yet recruiting
The study is approved but has not started enrolling yet.
Randomised
Participants are assigned by chance to one of the groups being compared, so the groups are similar and the comparison is fair.
Placebo
A treatment that looks like the real one but has no active effect. Not every trial uses one: the information sheet tells you.
Phase 1, 2, 3, 4
Trials of medicines go through phases: first safety in a few people, then effect and dose, then comparison with the usual treatment in many patients, then follow-up after approval.
Informed consent
Your written agreement to take part, after you have received and understood all the information about the study.

Please note. Clinical Research Chaos does not run, recommend or select clinical trials and does not give medical advice. This page only opens searches on public registries: nothing you type is sent to us. Registry listings are written by the study teams; always confirm details with the study contact and your doctor. Be wary of any study that asks you to pay to take part.
