Why P-Values Do Not Belong in Table 1

Few practices in medical publishing are as persistent as reporting P-values in Table 1.
In randomized trials, testing whether baseline differences arose by chance is illogical because randomization is known by design.
In observational cohorts, P-values confound sample size with clinical importance, overemphasizing trivial age differences while missing major confounders.
Methodologists and reporting guidelines like CONSORT and STROBE have advised against this practice for decades.
Have you ever had a peer reviewer demand that you add P-values into your Table 1?

Key points:
1. The Real Purpose of Table 1: Table 1 exists to outline patient traits rather than show formal hypothesis tests. Its true purpose is to summarize baseline features so readers can assess clinical comparability.
2. The Table 1 Fallacy in Randomized Trials: In randomized trials, a P-value tests whether treatment allocation occurred at random. Randomization guarantees this by definition, making significance testing of baseline balance completely illogical.
3. Sample Size Distorts the Results: P-values are sensitive to sample size. In huge cohorts, trivial differences produce significant P-values, while in small samples, true clinically meaningful imbalances remain hidden behind high P-values.
4. Guidelines Explicitly Oppose Them: Major guidelines like CONSORT for randomized trials and STROBE for observational studies advise against reporting P-values in baseline tables, urging researchers to avoid confusing description with inference.

Takeaway: Table 1 exists to describe patients, not test hypotheses; drop the P-values and evaluate clinical relevance.
